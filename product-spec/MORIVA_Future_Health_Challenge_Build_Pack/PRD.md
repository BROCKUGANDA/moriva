# MORIVA — Product Requirements Document (PRD)

**Project:** Future Health Digital Innovation Challenge 2026  
**Product:** MORIVA  
**Tagline:** Notice earlier. Find the right support.  
**Version:** 1.0 · **Prepared:** 8 November 2026  
**Initial geography:** Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates  
**Initial user scope:** Adults aged 18+  
**Product state:** Hackathon prototype specification, not a production clinical service

> **Critical boundary:** MORIVA is a proposed digital wellbeing recognition and care-navigation platform. The prototype may demonstrate consented self-assessment, explainable non-diagnostic results, provider discovery, consented referral workflows and simulated human follow-up. It must not claim regulatory approval, clinical validation in Abu Dhabi, live provider integration or live crisis-response capability unless independently evidenced.

---

# 0. Executive decision

Build a privacy-first, bilingual-ready, screen-to-care platform that helps an adult notice a possible change in wellbeing, understand the limits of a voluntary assessment, choose a proportionate support option, connect with a trusted provider and track whether the handoff actually happened.

The defining product innovation is **closed-loop care navigation**. Many digital wellbeing products stop after the questionnaire, content recommendation or chatbot answer. MORIVA structures the journey from self-recognition to a consent-authorised referral and a measurable follow-up event. The product measures whether a person reached appropriate support, not merely whether they opened an app.

## Product statement

MORIVA turns a voluntary wellbeing signal into an understandable, user-controlled path to appropriate human support, with explicit consent and auditable follow-through.

## One-line pitch

**MORIVA closes the gap between noticing a change in wellbeing and reaching trusted support.**

## Challenge-fit matrix

| Challenge requirement | MORIVA response | Evidence in the prototype |
|---|---|---|
| Validated early identification | Versioned assessment instruments with source, language, scoring and approval metadata | A user completes a configured instrument; the result explains what it can and cannot say |
| Stepped, local navigation | Clinically governed, deterministic pathway rules and a verified provider directory | Rationale-coded support choices, service eligibility and local filters |
| Human-led high-risk escalation | Explicit risk-signal protocol, emergency guidance, responder-availability model and fail-safe | A separate urgent-risk scenario; no false “handoff complete” state |
| Privacy, consent and autonomy | Granular, revocable consent and minimum-necessary sharing | Consent review, withdrawal, access log and export/deletion workflow |
| Physical + mental care coordination | Consent-controlled health-context summary and FHIR integration boundary | User-authorised context preview; no fake live EHR connection |
| Outcomes and safety | Referral lifecycle events, follow-up windows, waiting time, adverse-event reporting and subgroup monitoring | Dashboard distinguishes real, simulated and unavailable data |

## Demo-day proof requirements

1. The principal user journey works end-to-end without dead links or placeholder screens.
2. Every demo record is synthetic and labelled as such.
3. An instrument is used only if content rights, translation, scoring and clinical suitability are approved. Otherwise use an explicitly labelled synthetic assessment simulation.
4. Support navigation is deterministic and explainable, not generated by an LLM.
5. Referral states are real application states. A simulated provider workflow is visibly marked as a simulation.
6. Crisis guidance is accessible at all times and uses verified local sources. A mock clinician inbox is never described as an actual emergency responder.
7. Users control whether a result, note or context summary is shared.
8. Automated tests cover tenant isolation, consent enforcement, urgent-risk handling, idempotency, access control and the complete demonstration journey.

# 2. Problem definition and theory of change

## 2.1 User problem

A person may notice stress, reduced wellbeing, disrupted sleep, anxiety symptoms or changes in everyday functioning but be unsure what those signals mean or where to go. A screening result can increase awareness without producing a completed referral. Provider availability, eligibility, language, cost, stigma, wait time and fragmented records can interrupt the path to care.

## 2.2 System problem

The care journey crosses multiple actors: the individual, community support, primary care, mental-health professionals, emergency services and sometimes physical-health services. Information is fragmented, data-sharing permissions can be unclear, service directories become stale and there may be no reliable way to see whether a referral was accepted or completed.

## 2.3 Theory of change

If an adult can privately and voluntarily reflect on wellbeing using an appropriate, versioned instrument; understand the result without being labelled or diagnosed; choose a proportionate support option based on transparent criteria; share only the minimum information needed with a provider they select; and track the referral and follow-up, then MORIVA may reduce navigation friction and make the transition from screening to care more observable.

This is a hypothesis to be tested. A hackathon prototype cannot claim symptom reduction, earlier diagnosis, reduced suicide, reduced hospitalisation or clinical efficacy without suitable prospective evidence.

## 2.4 North-star metric

**Verified appropriate follow-up rate** = eligible referrals with a confirmed contact or follow-up event inside the configured follow-up window / all eligible referrals in the cohort and observation window.

“Confirmed” must identify its evidence source: provider acknowledgement, patient confirmation or authorised system event. The dashboard must show the source and the amount of missing data. A status generated by the demo simulator must never be presented as a real care outcome.

## 2.5 Supporting metrics

- Assessment start-to-completion rate.
- User comprehension of result limitations.
- Support-option selection rate.
- Referral initiation rate.
- Provider acknowledgement time.
- Time to first contact.
- Appointment scheduled rate.
- Follow-up completed within a configured window.
- User-reported usefulness and trust.
- User-reported change in wellbeing/functioning using the same approved instrument/version and measurement interval.
- Safety protocol completion and response latency where a real staffed service exists.
- Adverse events, near misses, false escalation and missed escalation found through review.
- Equity indicators by age band, gender, nationality, language and geography only where lawful, necessary, volunteered, consented and statistically safe to report.

Do not invent numerical performance targets. Establish baselines and targets with a clinical/service partner after observing real workflow data.

# 3. Scope, assumptions and non-goals

## 3.1 Initial scope

- Adults aged 18 and older.
- Abu Dhabi is the first configured geography.
- English-first implementation with architecture and layout support for Arabic/RTL.
- Voluntary, user-initiated check-ins and self-assessments; no passive sensing or covert monitoring.
- Versioned instrument framework; only clinically reviewed and legally cleared content may be enabled.
- Transparent result explanation; no diagnosis.
- Rule-based, clinically governed stepped support navigation.
- Curated provider/service directory with verification and last-checked metadata.
- User-controlled referral and minimum-necessary context sharing.
- Referral lifecycle tracking and optional reminders.
- Role-separated professional work queue for prototype demonstration.
- Persistent Help Now surface with region-configured official contacts.
- Privacy centre: consent, access, export and deletion-request flows.
- Synthetic demo tenant and synthetic patient scenarios.
- Operations dashboard for de-identified workflow outcomes and quality monitoring.

## 3.2 Explicit non-goals

- Direct diagnosis, treatment plans or prescribing.
- Autonomous clinical triage by AI.
- AI-only crisis detection or emergency dispatch.
- Automatic transfer of identifiable data to police, employers, family or providers without a valid reviewed basis and appropriate consent/legal exception.
- Live hospital/EHR connections without a real partner, contract, security review and tested integration.
- Real-time appointment inventory without a verified provider API.
- Telemedicine delivery or recording.
- Open peer-to-peer social network, public profiles, public wellbeing scores or leaderboards.
- Minors/pediatric pathways until safeguarding, guardian consent, clinical and legal requirements are designed and approved.
- Employer surveillance or individual-level employer reporting.
- Passive voice, location, wearable, keyboard, microphone, camera, email or social-media monitoring.
- Claims that MORIVA is a medical device, clinical provider or licensed mental-health service.
- Clinical recommendations based on nationality, gender, ethnicity or other protected attributes.

## 3.3 Assumptions to verify

- A qualified clinical advisor can review assessment and escalation protocols.
- A provider directory can be seeded with verified public listings for the prototype; simulated listings must be clearly labelled.
- No real referral receiving workflow exists without a signed partner and staffed coverage.
- UAE/Abu Dhabi health-data and digital-health requirements will be reviewed by qualified counsel and the competent health authority before live processing.
- Data residency, cross-border transfer, hosting and processor contracts will be determined before a live pilot.

# 4. Users, actors and permissions

## 4.1 Individual

A person who wants to reflect on wellbeing, understand an assessment, discover support and decide whether to share information. Owns the default view and controls sharing.

## 4.2 Licensed clinician / authorised care professional

A verified professional who can access only referrals or records assigned to them or their organisation, and only within documented consent/legal authority. Clinical decisions remain theirs. Credentials and scope must be verified before production access.

## 4.3 Provider organisation coordinator

Manages provider availability, service eligibility and referral acknowledgement within their organisation. Cannot read unrelated personal journals or unshared assessment responses.

## 4.4 Community support organisation

Provides a verified service entry, programme, information or resource. It receives no personal data unless the individual explicitly requests a referral and the organisation is approved to receive it.

## 4.5 MORIVA operations administrator

Manages provider verification, content status, resource freshness, language content, system health and aggregated operational reporting. Cannot access private journal content by default. Break-glass access is exceptional, ticketed, time-limited and audited.

## 4.6 Clinical governance administrator

Approves instrument versions, translations, pathway rules, escalation protocols and clinical content. This role must be distinct from a generic system administrator. Every approval records author, approver, date, evidence/source, version, effective date and rollback history.

## 4.7 Permission matrix

| Capability | Individual | Clinician | Provider coordinator | Ops admin | Clinical governance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| View own assessments | Yes | only shared/assigned | No | No | no routine access |
| View private journal | Yes | only explicitly shared | No | No | No |
| Create referral | Yes | authorised workflow | No | No | No |
| Acknowledge referral | No | Yes | Yes, limited | No | No |
| View shared context | Own | assigned/consented | minimum required | No | no routine access |
| Manage provider listing | No | limited profile | own org | Yes | review clinical scope |
| Approve instrument | No | recommend | No | No | Yes |
| Configure pathway rules | No | propose | No | no clinical approval | Yes |
| View aggregate metrics | own only | authorised cohort | own service aggregate | Yes, thresholded | Yes, thresholded |
| Inspect audit trail | own access history | relevant records | own organisation | security-scoped | governance-scoped |

Apply least privilege and deny by default. Enforce permissions in backend services and database policies, not only by hiding UI elements.

# 5. Core user journeys

## Journey A — Voluntary check-in to clear next step

1. User arrives on MORIVA landing page.
2. User sees the boundary: support and navigation, not diagnosis or emergency care.
3. User selects language and creates an account or uses a permitted guest flow.
4. User reviews a plain-language privacy summary and gives separate consent for account, assessment, optional AI, referral and health-context sharing.
5. User starts an approved instrument. Before the first question, show purpose, timeframe, estimated duration, optionality and what happens if a concerning response is selected.
6. User completes or saves/resumes. No answer is silently discarded.
7. Assessment engine calculates the score using the exact approved instrument version and deterministic scoring implementation.
8. Result page explains what it means, what it cannot establish, instrument version, completion time and next options.
9. Rule-based navigation displays options with rationale, eligibility, location, language, accessibility and verified cost/coverage details where available.
10. User chooses whether to create a referral and reviews an exact share preview.
11. User confirms fields and recipient; system records consent and creates an idempotent referral.
12. User sees an honest status. If the provider is a demo seed, a persistent “SIMULATED PROVIDER” label appears.
13. Follow-up reminder occurs only under chosen notification preferences.
14. User can revoke future sharing and request deletion, subject to disclosed legal retention obligations.

## Journey B — Self-guided or community support

1. Result page presents a proportionate selection of support options.
2. User chooses a resource or community programme.
3. User sees source, author/organisation, eligibility, language, availability, accessibility and verification date.
4. Opening a public resource does not share identity or assessment data.
5. A community referral is a separate action with consent preview.
6. User can mark “I contacted them” without proof; UI calls it user-reported, not provider-verified.

## Journey C — Provider handoff

1. User opens a provider profile and reviews verification status and service information.
2. User chooses a service and initiates referral.
3. Referral is created as `PENDING_PROVIDER_ACKNOWLEDGEMENT`.
4. Provider inbox receives minimum necessary referral data only if consent and provider eligibility are valid.
5. Provider acknowledges, requests more information through a controlled consent flow, declines with a safe reason category, or marks appointment scheduled.
6. User receives status update and next steps.
7. User follow-up is recorded separately from provider confirmation.
8. Provider event and patient report are stored as different evidence types.

## Journey D — Urgent-risk disclosure

1. User explicitly endorses a risk-related response or selects Help Now.
2. Application immediately pauses routine navigation and any AI response.
3. It displays clear, location-appropriate emergency guidance from a verified, versioned configuration.
4. For Abu Dhabi prototype configuration, the Abu Dhabi Public Health Center guidance says to call 999 or go to the nearest emergency department for emergencies involving thoughts of harming self/others or severe disorganised behaviour. The DoH 24/7 800-SAKINA / 800-725462 mental-health support hotline is an additional support route, not a substitute for emergency services.
5. User is offered a direct call action and nearest emergency department instruction. Do not bury this in a chatbot.
6. If a real staffed and approved responder channel exists, offer it with operating hours, response expectation and exact data to be shared. If it does not exist, do not display “we have alerted a professional”.
7. Any incident record is access-restricted, audited and governed by an approved protocol.
8. A positive response never automatically results in a diagnosis, law-enforcement report or employer notification.
9. AI is not the sole risk detector and cannot clear a user as safe.

## Journey E — Consented physical + mental context sharing

1. User opens Health Context.
2. App explains that only selected information is included.
3. User selects fields from self-reported context; imported records require source, consent and verification metadata.
4. App previews the exact summary and recipient.
5. User grants a time-bounded or purpose-bounded permission.
6. Clinician accesses only the shared summary for the assigned referral.
7. Consent withdrawal stops future access and triggers downstream notification where supported. It cannot retroactively erase a lawful record that must be retained; the app explains this accurately.

## Journey F — Privacy control

1. User views each consent purpose, recipient, data categories, grant date and current status.
2. User revokes optional consent.
3. Application records revocation and blocks future disclosure under that permission.
4. User requests data export or deletion.
5. System validates identity and executes or queues the request according to a configured and legally reviewed retention schedule.
6. User receives auditable request status. Never claim deletion is complete while copies remain in backups, legal holds or downstream systems.

# 8. Clinical content and assessment framework

## 8.1 Non-diagnostic boundary

MORIVA supports self-reflection, screening and navigation. It does not make diagnoses or treatment decisions. Only licensed professionals within scope can interpret clinical context and decide care.

## 8.2 Instrument registry

Each assessment instrument must have immutable version metadata:

- `instrument_id`, name, owner/source, construct, intended population, language, version, release date, content checksum, licence/permission evidence, item set, response scale, timeframe, scoring implementation version, missing-response rules, interpretation reference, clinical approver, translator/reviewer, validity evidence, limitations, effective date and retirement date.
- Responses link to the exact instrument version, not a mutable current record.
- Scoring code has table-driven unit tests for boundary values and missing-data cases.
- Any question/translation/scoring change creates a new version and requires approval.

## 8.3 Candidate instruments for clinical review

- **WHO-5 Well-Being Index:** self-reported wellbeing over the previous two weeks. WHO lists multiple translations, including Arabic and English. The WHO-5 site directs copyright queries to WHO; confirm current permissions and conditions before embedding wording in a commercial product.
- **PHQ-2 / PHQ-9:** depression symptom screening instruments. Public-domain reproduction information is available from established sources, but the clinical team must select the appropriate version, use context, interpretation and response protocol.
- **GAD-2 / GAD-7:** anxiety symptom screening instruments. Confirm source, version, language and local population suitability.

Do not claim that an instrument is validated for every Abu Dhabi nationality, language or care setting merely because it has been validated elsewhere. Arabic translation availability is not identical to local psychometric validation. Do not machine-translate instrument wording at runtime. Use reviewed translations and record provenance.

## 8.4 Hackathon implementation approach

- Build the instrument registry and deterministic scoring infrastructure.
- Configure only an instrument/version approved by a qualified clinical advisor and whose reproduction terms are confirmed.
- If approval is unavailable by demo day, use an explicitly labelled **synthetic assessment simulation** and do not display it as a validated questionnaire.
- Use synthetic data to exercise thresholds, partial completion and urgent-risk branches.

## 8.5 Result explanation content

Result pages use authored and clinically reviewed content blocks, not free-form LLM diagnosis. Each block includes author, reviewer, source, version, locale, publication date and review date.

# 9. Stepped-care navigation rules

## 9.1 Model

Support a least-intensive-safe-option concept while never withholding higher-level care a user requests or a clinician considers appropriate. The pathway is a menu with explanation, not a coercive gate.

Potential layers:

1. Self-guided evidence-informed information and optional exercises.
2. Community support or structured non-clinical programme.
3. Primary healthcare assessment.
4. Qualified tele-mental-health or in-person mental-health care.
5. Specialist or urgent clinical care.
6. Emergency response.

## 9.2 Rule engine

- Use deterministic rules authored and versioned by clinical governance.
- Allowed inputs: approved assessment result, explicit risk response, user-selected preference, geography, age eligibility, service eligibility, accessibility and language.
- Demographic attributes must not infer clinical severity or eligibility unless a validated and legally approved clinical rule explicitly requires it.
- AI output cannot modify care tier.
- User can choose a more intensive available option, ask a human for help or decline.
- Every output includes rule version and rationale identifiers.
- Missing/unknown inputs must not be interpreted as “low risk”.
- If rules fail, show neutral general options and Help Now; do not default to “no support needed”.

## 9.3 Example pathway configuration

This is an implementation shape, not a clinically approved triage table.

| Signal | System action | Human authority |
|---|---|---|
| No urgent signal; user wants information | Show self-guided and community options, plus primary-care choice | User chooses |
| Approved screening indicates follow-up may be useful | Explain result and offer primary-care/qualified service options | Clinician assesses |
| User requests specialist care | Show eligible verified specialist options | Provider determines appropriateness |
| Explicit immediate danger or self-harm/other-harm disclosure | Immediately show emergency guidance and stop routine AI/navigation flow | Emergency/professional responders act |
| Instrument incomplete or unavailable | Explain no result can be calculated; offer general support options | Human if user requests |
| Clinical rule version expired | Disable rule and use safe generic navigation | Clinical governance reviews |

No threshold values are specified here. They require clinical approval and documented protocol.

# 10. Crisis and high-risk safety specification

## 10.1 Core principle

Urgent safety is a deterministic, auditable workflow independent of generative AI. LLMs may never be the only mechanism for detecting or responding to suicidal intent, self-harm, harm to others or an acute emergency.

## 10.2 Trigger classes

- Explicit user selection of Help Now.
- Explicit endorsement of a clinically designated urgent-risk item.
- Direct user statement to the support assistant indicating immediate danger, subject to a deterministic safety policy; uncertain cases must surface Help Now.
- Staff activation of an incident workflow.

Passive surveillance is prohibited.

## 10.3 Required behaviour

- Immediately stop normal AI generation and show a static, tested safety surface.
- For Abu Dhabi, the Abu Dhabi Public Health Center currently instructs people in emergency situations involving thoughts of harming self/others or severe disorganised behaviour to call 999 or go to the nearest emergency department. Verify source and contact before each release.
- Also display the Department of Health Abu Dhabi 24/7 mental-health support hotline: `800-SAKINA` / `800-725462` as an additional support route, not a substitute for emergency care.
- Present call actions prominently with `tel:` links where supported and visible digits.
- “I have contacted help” is user-reported state, not a clinical risk clearance.
- Offer a human channel only if real service coverage is configured and verified. Include hours, response expectation and exact data to be shared.
- If offline, cached verified emergency copy remains available.
- Never depend on AI, API or directory availability to display emergency instructions.

## 10.4 Fail-safe behaviours

- No known geography: ask user to choose a location; show general nearest-emergency-department advice.
- Emergency configuration expired: display last verified emergency instructions with internal expiry warning and alert operations owner; do not invent a replacement number.
- No responder on duty: do not offer callback/handoff.
- Failed notification: mark `NOT_DELIVERED`; show direct contact instructions.
- AI provider down: safety UI remains fully functional.
- User exits: emergency entry remains persistent; do not trap the user.

## 10.5 Incident governance

Every incident rule/config has owner, clinical approver, jurisdiction, version, source, review date, escalation owner, staff coverage, retention and audit. # 11. Trust, consent, privacy and data governance

## 11.1 Privacy principles

- Voluntary, informed, specific and revocable consent where consent is the appropriate legal basis.
- Collect minimum necessary information.
- No covert collection or background signals.
- No data sale, advertising profiling or cross-context ad targeting using wellbeing data.
- No individual-level reporting to employers, schools, sponsors, insurers or family by default.
- No training of public/shared AI models on sensitive user data.
- Keep product analytics free of questionnaire answers, journal content, free-text crisis details and health identifiers.
- Separate identity, care content, operational telemetry and de-identified analytics.
- Complete privacy impact and clinical safety assessments before production.

## 11.2 Consent purposes

Each purpose is independently versioned:

- Account/security processing.
- Wellbeing assessment storage.
- Optional journal storage.
- Optional AI processing and external model transfer.
- Named referral to a named provider/service.
- Health-context sharing with named provider.
- Optional follow-up notifications.
- Optional research use (separate ethics/governance route).
- Marketing (not bundled with care consent).

## 11.3 Consent data model

A consent record includes subject, purpose, data categories, recipient, jurisdiction, wording version, legal basis, granted/revoked timestamps, source, expiry, scope, status, proof metadata and downstream notification state. Consent is checked server-side at every read, write, export and disclosure.

## 11.4 Privacy-rights workflows

- View/export.
- Correction request.
- Delete request.
- Consent withdrawal.
- Restrict future sharing.
- Object to optional processing where applicable.
- Request status and appeal/escalation where required.

A deletion request cannot promise erasure of data retained under a legal obligation or from immutable security records. Explain limits and delete/anonymise when permitted.

## 11.5 UAE and Abu Dhabi compliance workstream

The prototype includes a compliance register, not a claim of compliance. Qualified local counsel and the competent health authority must determine applicability of at least:

- UAE Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on Personal Data Protection, including lawful basis, controller/processor responsibilities, data-subject rights and cross-border considerations.
- Federal Law No. 2 of 2019 on use of ICT in health fields and implementing requirements for health-data confidentiality and security.
- Abu Dhabi Department of Health digital-health policies, health-data exchange standards, provider/licensing requirements, telehealth requirements, mental-health governance policy, and applicable health information exchange/Malaffi participation rules.
- Data residency, cross-border transfer, cloud region, subcontractor and data export obligations.
- Medical device/software as a medical device classification if intended purpose, claims or functionality bring the product within scope.
- Advertising, health-content, professional scope and clinical referral rules.

This PRD is not legal advice and does not establish that the prototype is cleared for clinical deployment.

# 14. Referral lifecycle and state machine

## 14.1 Canonical states

`DRAFT` → `AWAITING_USER_CONSENT` → `READY_TO_SEND` → `PENDING_PROVIDER_ACKNOWLEDGEMENT` → one of `ACKNOWLEDGED`, `NEEDS_INFORMATION`, `DECLINED`, `PROVIDER_UNAVAILABLE`.

Further states where appropriate: `APPOINTMENT_OFFERED`, `APPOINTMENT_SCHEDULED`, `CONTACT_CONFIRMED`, `FOLLOW_UP_DUE`, `FOLLOW_UP_COMPLETED`, `CLOSED`.

Terminal/exception states: `CANCELLED_BY_USER`, `EXPIRED`, `DELIVERY_FAILED`, `WITHDRAWN_CONSENT`.

## 14.2 Transition rules

- Every transition validates current state, actor role, consent, recipient verification and expected version.
- Use optimistic concurrency (`version` column) to reject stale updates.
- Each transition writes immutable `referral_event` and audit event in a transaction/outbox boundary.
- Do not allow `PENDING_PROVIDER_ACKNOWLEDGEMENT` to jump to `FOLLOW_UP_COMPLETED` without evidence.
- `CONTACT_CONFIRMED` stores evidence source: provider-confirmed, patient-reported or integration-confirmed.
- If consent is revoked before sending, block the disclosure and mark the referral `WITHDRAWN_CONSENT`.
- Retry after an unknown HTTP result queries idempotency status before creating another referral.

## 14.3 Reminder policy

Reminders are configurable by purpose and timezone. Use minimal, non-sensitive notification text (“You have an update in MORIVA”), not “Your anxiety referral…”. Reminder windows are configured with a service partner and clinical governance, not invented by engineering.

Do not split into microservices during the hackathon. Boundaries should permit later extraction where scale or regulation requires it.

- React with accessible component primitives.

# 17. Error catalogue and recovery behaviour

All errors are logged with correlation ID, code and safe metadata. Sensitive data is redacted. Client messages are human-readable and never contain stack traces.

|---|---:|---|---|---|
| `AUTH_REQUIRED` | 401 | Session missing/expired | Sign in again; preserve non-sensitive navigation | After auth |
| `AUTH_FORBIDDEN` | 403 | Role/scope denied | “You don’t have access to this information.” | No |
| `CONSENT_REQUIRED` | 403 | Purpose consent absent | Show exact consent request | After consent |
| `CONSENT_REVOKED` | 403 | Permission withdrawn | Stop disclosure; explain revoke state | No until new consent |
| `INSTRUMENT_NOT_APPROVED` | 409 | Assessment version not approved | Disable instrument; offer general support | No |
| `INSTRUMENT_VERSION_EXPIRED` | 409 | Version retired/expired | Do not start/complete under stale config | No |
| `ASSESSMENT_INCOMPLETE` | 422 | Required items missing | Explain requirement; save progress | After correction |
| `SCORING_CONFIG_INVALID` | 500 | Scoring config integrity issue | No score; safe fallback | Only after operator fix |
| `URGENT_SUPPORT_TRIGGERED` | flow | User needs urgent support guidance | Static Help Now flow | Never retry model |
| `PATHWAY_UNAVAILABLE` | 503 | Rules unavailable/expired | Generic choices + Help Now | Backoff, safe fallback |
| `PROVIDER_UNVERIFIED` | 409 | Provider not eligible | Block new referral; show alternatives | No |
| `PROVIDER_SERVICE_EXPIRED` | 409 | Listing expired | Exclude service | No |
| `REFERRAL_DUPLICATE` | 409 | Same idempotency key/intent | Return existing referral state | Query only |
| `REFERRAL_STATE_CONFLICT` | 409 | Stale transition | Refresh current state | No blind retry |
| `REFERRAL_DELIVERY_UNKNOWN` | 202 | Delivery status uncertain | “Checking status”; reconcile by key | Query, then retry safely |
| `REFERRAL_DELIVERY_FAILED` | 503 | Confirmed delivery failure | Show not sent; offer safe retry | Backoff |
| `PROVIDER_UNAVAILABLE` | 503 | Provider integration unavailable | Other options; retain draft | Backoff |
| `AI_DISABLED` | 403 | AI feature not approved/enabled | Non-AI path | No |

# 18. Reliability, retries and distributed workflow

- Use a transactional outbox when database state changes require notification/integration delivery.
- Use unique idempotency constraints for referral creation, consent capture and external delivery.
- Retry only idempotent operations. Use exponential backoff with full jitter and a bounded attempt count.
- Respect provider `Retry-After` headers and quotas.
- Use a circuit breaker for external dependencies; fallback to cached verified data where safe.
- Do not retry emergency contact/call actions automatically.
- Distinguish request accepted, queued, sent, acknowledged and completed.
- A queue outage must not make Help Now inaccessible.
- Add dead-letter handling, operator visibility and audited replay controls.
- Reconcile delivery-unknown referrals by querying external status before attempting duplicate submission.
- Store timestamps in UTC; calculate user-facing windows in configured local timezone.
- Queue payloads must not contain unnecessary clinical text and must follow retention policy.

# 19. Security requirements

Minimum engineering baseline: OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 as a verification framework; threat model and security tests are required, not a logo or checkbox claim.

- TLS 1.2+ (prefer TLS 1.3).
- Encrypt database/storage backups and sensitive fields.
- Secrets only in a secret manager/environment, never repository or browser bundle.
- Secure, HTTP-only, same-site cookies; CSRF controls where applicable.
- CSP, strict CORS, security headers and output encoding.
- Parameterised SQL/ORM and validated user inputs.
- Rate limiting at authentication, assessment, referral and AI endpoints.
- MFA for staff, least privilege, short staff sessions and session revocation.
- Object-level authorisation on every endpoint.
- Tenant/org scoping tested in database and service layers.
- Prevent IDOR/BOLA; never trust client-supplied user or organisation ID.
- Audit access to health data, consent changes, exports, clinician reads, provider edits and admin actions.
- Redact logs, traces and analytics; do not log questionnaire answers or prompts by default.
- Dependency scanning, lockfiles, SBOM and secret scanning.
- Encrypted backups and tested restore procedure.
- Change approval and break-glass controls for production access.
- No production deployment with debug routes, demo credentials, seeded patient accounts or sample clinical data enabled.

# 20. AI component requirements

## 20.1 Purpose

Optional reflection and information-navigation assistant. The model is a conversational interface to approved content, not a clinical decision engine.

## 20.2 Allowed tasks

- Explain MORIVA's own non-clinical workflows.
- Explain approved educational content with source attribution.
- Help users organise questions for a clinician.
- Offer neutral reflective prompts that do not claim therapeutic efficacy.
- Summarise user-selected content for the user, with confirmation before saving or sharing.

## 20.3 Prohibited tasks

- Diagnosis, treatment recommendation, medication or dose advice.
- Score or reinterpret validated instruments.
- Infer user risk from hidden/passive signals.
- Decide care tier or mark a crisis as resolved.
- Autonomous referral or disclosure.
- Claim emergency monitoring or human supervision when not staffed.
- Generate fabricated providers, prices, availability or helpline numbers.

## 20.4 Architecture

- `AI_REFLECTION_ENABLED=false` by default.
- Consent check before each model request.
- Context minimisation and PII redaction.
- Approved-content retrieval only; cite content record/version in UI.
- Input safety check before model; output safety check after model.
- Hard response timeout, token budget, request limit and cost cap.
- Prompt version and model identifier logged without raw sensitive text unless explicitly necessary and approved.
- Model provider contract and data-retention terms reviewed before live use.
- Fail closed if policy, consent or safety service is unavailable.
- No autonomous tool calling that creates referrals, shares data, changes consent, notifies third parties or edits clinical records.

## 20.5 Evaluation

Build a synthetic evaluation suite covering benign reflection, diagnosis requests, medication requests, self-harm, harm to others, abuse disclosure, hallucinated provider/phone, prompt injection, multilingual/code-switching inputs, ambiguous urgency, user withdrawal of consent and provider outage. Record expected safe behaviour and block feature activation on critical failure.

# 22. Analytics, outcomes and fairness

## 22.1 Funnel events

`assessment_started`, `assessment_completed`, `result_viewed`, `support_option_viewed`, `support_option_selected`, `referral_created`, `referral_delivered`, `provider_acknowledged`, `appointment_scheduled`, `contact_confirmed`, `follow_up_due`, `follow_up_completed`, `referral_closed`.

Do not include question text, score values, journal content or names in general product analytics. Clinical outcome data belongs in governed tables and can be aggregated only after privacy review.

## 22.2 Data quality

Every dashboard identifies whether a status came from the patient, provider, integrated system or demo simulator. Missing confirmation is “unknown”, not assumed success or failure.

## 22.3 Fairness

- Compare access, wait times, referral completion and missingness across approved cohorts.
- Avoid protected attributes in navigation unless clinically justified and lawful.
- Suppress small subgroups using configurable minimum cell sizes.
- Investigate measurement bias, language effects, service capacity differences and differential non-response.
- Do not claim algorithmic fairness based only on balanced charts.

# 23. Business model and deployment pathway

## 23.1 Commercial hypotheses

- B2B2C implementation/licensing with health systems and primary-care networks.
- Provider/health-network coordination subscription where allowed and without pay-to-rank clinical access.
- Public-health programme deployment with service-level reporting.
- Employer-sponsored access only with strict separation: employers receive only safe, thresholded aggregates, never individual records or identifiable risk details.
- Optional patient premium features only if they do not create safety/access barriers; core emergency and essential support information stays free.

## 23.2 Never monetise

- Sensitive individual wellbeing profiles.
- Advertising, data brokerage or lead resale.
- Higher provider placement in clinically governed results.
- Crisis access.
- Referral completion incentives that distort clinical choice.

## 23.3 Pilot gates

1. Clinical advisor and named accountable clinical governance.
2. Legal/regulatory review for intended purpose and geography.
3. Data protection impact assessment and threat model.
4. Provider verification and signed receiving workflow.
5. Crisis protocol, trained human coverage and tabletop test.
6. Instrument rights, translations, thresholds and scoring approved.
7. Security testing and restore test.
8. Accessibility and Arabic language review.
9. Outcome definitions and baseline plan approved.
10. Incident response and user support staffed.

No public production claims until these gates are complete.

# 25. Demo script (6–8 minutes)

1. **Set the boundary (30 sec):** MORIVA is not a diagnosis engine; it connects voluntary wellbeing recognition to appropriate support.
2. **Individual (90 sec):** Open synthetic adult account and show privacy-first onboarding/separate consent.
3. **Assessment (60 sec):** Complete approved instrument or labelled simulation; show result limitations and exact version.
4. **Navigation (60 sec):** Select support option; show provider verification, language/eligibility and “why this option”.
5. **Consent and referral (90 sec):** Preview exactly what is shared; create one idempotent referral.
6. **Human workspace (60 sec):** Switch to simulated provider role; acknowledge referral and schedule follow-up. Keep `SIMULATED` visible.
7. **Outcome (45 sec):** Show funnel and distinguish simulated/provider-confirmed/patient-reported events.
8. **Urgent scenario (60 sec):** Trigger direct urgent-risk response; show static emergency instructions and explain that no fake responder is claimed.
9. **Close (30 sec):** State pilot gates and measurable outcome hypothesis.

# 30. Final product rule

**MORIVA is successful only when it helps a person move from a voluntary signal to a clear, safe, user-controlled next step, and can honestly show whether the next step happened.** Screening volume, AI message volume and attractive dashboards are not substitutes for trusted care access, consent, human oversight and measurable follow-through.
